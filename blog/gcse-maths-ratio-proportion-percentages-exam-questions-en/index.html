GCSE Maths Ratio, Proportion and Percentages: Exam Questions Step by Step

GCSE Maths ratio, proportion and percentage exam questions explained step by step, including reverse percentages, compound change and common mark-losing errors.

Ratio, proportion and percentage questions reward structure more than speed. A GCSE exam can hide the same calculation inside a recipe, a scale drawing, a sale price or a population model. The reliable first move is to decide whether the quantities change by a fixed amount, a fixed multiplier or a stated ratio.

Direct answer. Use a multiplier for percentage change, a unitary method or scale factor for direct proportion, and check whether the total number of ratio parts matches the quantity you are splitting.

Ratio: split the total into parts first

Divide £420 in the ratio 3:43:43:4. The total number of parts is:

3+4=7.3+4=7.3+4=7.

One part is 420÷7=60420\div 7=60420÷7=60. The two shares are therefore:

3×60=180,4×60=240.3\times60=180,\qquad 4\times60=240.3×60=180,4×60=240.

Always add the final shares back together. It catches a surprising number of calculator and transcription mistakes.

Direct and inverse proportion

When yyy is directly proportional to xxx:

y=kx.y=kx.y=kx.

If y=18y=18y=18 when x=6x=6x=6, then k=3k=3k=3, so y=3xy=3xy=3x. A doubling of xxx doubles yyy.

For inverse proportion:

y=kx.y=\frac{k}{x}.y=xk​.

If six workers take ten hours at the same rate, twelve workers take five hours. The product of the paired values stays constant; it is not a direct-proportion situation just because both numbers appear in the question.

Percentage increase and decrease: work with multipliers

A 15% increase means multiply by:

1+0.15=1.15.1+0.15=1.15.1+0.15=1.15.

A 15% decrease means multiply by 0.850.850.85. For compound change, multiply repeatedly. An £800 value that rises by 4% for three years is:

800×1.043.800\times1.04^3.800×1.043.

Do not add 4+4+44+4+44+4+4 to the original percentage. Each year's change applies to the value after the previous year.

Reverse percentages: divide by the multiplier

A coat costs £69 after a 20% reduction. The sale price represents 80% of the original, so:

original price=690.80=86.25.\text{original price}=\frac{69}{0.80}=86.25.original price=0.8069​=86.25.

Multiplying £69 by 1.201.201.20 would be wrong: it treats the reduced price as though it were the original value.

Exam-style question: percentage change after a discount

A phone is reduced by 25% and then increased by 25%. Is it back at its original price?

Start with £100. After the reduction:

100×0.75=75.100\times0.75=75.100×0.75=75.

After the increase:

75×1.25=93.75.75\times1.25=93.75.75×1.25=93.75.

The final price is 6.25% below the original. Equal percentage rises and falls do not cancel because they act on different starting values.

A short checklist before you commit an answer

  1. 1.Have you identified the original amount?
  2. 2.Is this a ratio, direct proportion, inverse proportion or percentage multiplier question?
  3. 3.Are the units compatible?
  4. 4.Does an increase give a sensible larger result?
  5. 5.For a reverse percentage, have you divided by the multiplier?
