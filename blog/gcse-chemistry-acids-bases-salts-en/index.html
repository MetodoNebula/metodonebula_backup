Acid and base questions become confusing when each reaction is memorised as a separate recipe. Most of the topic follows one idea: acids provide H+\mathrm{H^+}H+ ions in solution and alkalis provide OH−\mathrm{OH^-}OH− ions.

Direct answer. Neutralisation removes acidic and alkaline properties through H++OH−→H2O\mathrm{H^++OH^-\to H_2O}H++OH−→H2​O. To prepare a salt, identify the acid supplying the anion and the metal, oxide, hydroxide or carbonate supplying the cation.

Reading the pH scale

In the GCSE model:

  • pH<7pH<7pH<7: acidic.
  • pH=7pH=7pH=7: neutral.
  • pH>7pH>7pH>7: alkaline.

A fall of one pH unit represents roughly ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration. pH 2 is therefore not merely “slightly” more acidic than pH 3.

Four reaction patterns

acid+metal→salt+H2\text{acid}+\text{metal}\to\text{salt}+\mathrm{H_2}acid+metal→salt+H2​
acid+oxide or hydroxide→salt+H2O\text{acid}+\text{oxide or hydroxide}\to\text{salt}+\mathrm{H_2O}acid+oxide or hydroxide→salt+H2​O
acid+carbonate→salt+H2O+CO2\text{acid}+\text{carbonate}\to\text{salt}+\mathrm{H_2O}+\mathrm{CO_2}acid+carbonate→salt+H2​O+CO2​

The gas depends on the reactant: carbonates give carbon dioxide, while suitable metals give hydrogen.

Naming the salt

  • Hydrochloric acid →\to→ chloride.
  • Sulfuric acid →\to→ sulfate.
  • Nitric acid →\to→ nitrate.

For example:

H2SO4+CuO→CuSO4+H2O.\mathrm{H_2SO_4+CuO\to CuSO_4+H_2O}.H2​SO4​+CuO→CuSO4​+H2​O.

The acid provides sulfate and copper oxide provides copper.

Preparing a soluble salt

To make copper sulfate crystals:

  1. 1.Warm dilute sulfuric acid gently.
  2. 2.Add excess copper oxide until no more reacts.
  3. 3.Filter away the remaining solid.
  4. 4.Evaporate some water.
  5. 5.Cool to crystallise.
  6. 6.Dry the crystals.

The excess ensures all acid is used; filtration removes the insoluble excess.

Indicators and end point

An indicator changes colour across a pH range; it does not neutralise the solution. In a titration, the observed end point should be close to the chemical equivalence point.

Ionic equations and spectator ions

For hydrochloric acid reacting with sodium hydroxide:

HCl+NaOH→NaCl+H2O,\mathrm{HCl+NaOH\to NaCl+H_2O},HCl+NaOH→NaCl+H2​O,

the Na+\mathrm{Na^+}Na+ and Cl−\mathrm{Cl^-}Cl− ions remain unchanged. Removing them gives the net ionic equation:

H+(aq)+OH−(aq)→H2O(l).\mathrm{H^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)\to H_2O(l)}.H+(aq)+OH−(aq)→H2​O(l).

State symbols help to show which species are actually dissolved. A spectator ion still matters when identifying the final salt, but it does not undergo the chemical change in neutralisation.

Titration as an assessable procedure

Rinse the pipette with the solution it will measure and the burette with the solution it will contain. Record the initial meniscus reading at eye level, add only a few indicator drops to the flask and approach the end point one drop at a time. A rough trial locates the end point; further runs should produce concordant titres, commonly within 0.100.100.10–0.20 cm30.20\ \mathrm{cm^3}0.20 cm3 depending on the stated criterion.

The titre is final reading minus initial reading. Repeating a method without controlling the meniscus does not remove systematic error, and too much indicator can affect the result. A strong improvement names the change and explains why it increases precision.

Common mistakes

  • Writing water as a gas in neutralisation.
  • Treating every base as an alkali: an alkali is a soluble base.
  • Choosing the wrong acid for the required salt.
  • Forgetting to filter off excess solid.
  • Using cm3\mathrm{cm^3}cm3 directly in n=cVn=cVn=cV.
