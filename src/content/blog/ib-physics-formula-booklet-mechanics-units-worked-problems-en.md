The IB Physics formula booklet is useful only when each symbol is connected to a physical model. A formula does not decide whether a force is external, whether a velocity has a sign, or whether energy is conserved in the system you chose.

> **Direct answer.** Before substituting values, define the system, choose a positive direction and write every quantity in SI units. Then use the formula whose assumptions match the situation and check the final unit and sign.

## Forces and acceleration

Newton's second law is

$$
\sum F=ma.
$$

The sum is a vector sum. If right is positive, a 12 N driving force and 5 N resistive force give $\sum F=12-5=7$ N. For a 2 kg object, $a=7/2=3.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

The equation does not say that every force equals $ma$. It says the **resultant** force does. Draw the free-body diagram first whenever several forces act.

## Work, energy and power

For a constant force parallel to displacement,

$$
W=Fs.
$$

More generally, $W=Fs\cos\theta$. The angle is between the force and the displacement, not automatically the angle drawn in the diagram.

Kinetic and gravitational potential energy are

$$
E_k=\frac12mv^2,\qquad \Delta E_p=mg\Delta h.
$$

If a 0.50 kg object rises 3.0 m, its gravitational potential energy changes by approximately $0.50\times9.81\times3.0=14.7$ J. This says nothing yet about its final speed; you must account for every relevant transfer.

## Momentum and impulse

Momentum is

$$
p=mv,
$$

and impulse is the change in momentum:

$$
F\Delta t=\Delta p.
$$

For collisions in an isolated system, total momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions, so do not assume it without justification.

## Formula-booklet checks

- Convert kilometres per hour to metres per second before using mechanics equations.
- Keep velocity signs in one dimension; a negative result may be physically correct.
- Use newtons, joules, kilograms and seconds consistently.
- State the system before claiming conservation of energy or momentum.
