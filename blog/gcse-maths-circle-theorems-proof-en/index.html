GCSE Circle Theorems: How to Recognise the Diagram and Justify Every Angle

Master GCSE circle theorems: angles at the centre, same segment, diameter, tangents, cyclic quadrilaterals and geometric proofs.

Circle theorems are not mastered by memorising seven isolated diagrams. A difficult question changes the orientation, adds auxiliary lines and combines two results. The effective strategy is to locate the centre, radii, chords, diameters and tangents before calculating.

Direct answer. First mark which points lie on the circumference. Then identify the same chord or arc and write a geometrical reason for every step. An unsupported value can lose marks even when it is correct.

Angle at the centre and angle at the circumference

The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference subtending the same arc:

∠AOB=2∠ACB.\angle AOB=2\angle ACB.∠AOB=2∠ACB.

The phrase “same arc” is essential. It is not enough for both angles to sit inside the same circle. Draw radii to the chord endpoints if the relationship is not immediately visible.

Angles in the same segment

Two inscribed angles subtended by the same chord are equal. Both view the same arc from points in the same region of the circumference.

If they lie in opposite segments, the relationship changes: they become opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral and add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Angle in a semicircle

If a chord is a diameter, the angle it subtends at the circumference is a right angle:

∠ACB=90∘.\angle ACB=90^\circ.∠ACB=90∘.

Before applying this result, there must be evidence that the line passes through the centre. A chord that merely looks long is not necessarily a diameter.

Cyclic quadrilateral

In a quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on the circumference, opposite angles add to:

α+γ=180∘.\alpha+\gamma=180^\circ.α+γ=180∘.

You can also use the exterior angle: it equals the opposite interior angle. Check that all four points genuinely lie on the circle before quoting the theorem.

Radius, tangent and alternate segment theorem

A radius is perpendicular to a tangent at the point of contact. This creates a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle that often unlocks the solution.

The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment subtended by that chord. To avoid choosing the wrong angle, mark the shared chord first.

The two tangents from the same external point have equal lengths. This result creates isosceles triangles and links their angles.

Chained example

Suppose ABABAB is a diameter and a tangent touches the circle at BBB. If ∠BAC=34∘\angle BAC=34^\circ∠BAC=34∘, then:

∠ACB=90∘\angle ACB=90^\circ∠ACB=90∘

because it is the angle in a semicircle. From the angle sum of a triangle:

∠ABC=180∘−90∘−34∘=56∘.\angle ABC=180^\circ-90^\circ-34^\circ=56^\circ.∠ABC=180∘−90∘−34∘=56∘.

The angle between the tangent at BBB and BCBCBC is also 34∘34^\circ34∘ by the alternate segment theorem. Each calculation line uses a different reason.

How to write a proof

A clear proof alternates statement and justification:

  1. 1.Add radii when they create isosceles triangles.
  2. 2.Name angles with three letters to remove ambiguity.
  3. 3.Use basic triangle and parallel-line facts where required.
  4. 4.Quote the exact circle theorem.
  5. 5.Check that the result is consistent with the diagram without treating the diagram as a measurement.
