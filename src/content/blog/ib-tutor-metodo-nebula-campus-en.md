## What happens before the first topic is taught

The first useful question is not “what grade do you want?” but “what does the next assessment actually require?”. For IB Mathematics, that means distinguishing AA from AI and SL from HL before choosing practice. In Physics and Chemistry, it means seeing whether the difficulty lies in the model, the calculation, data analysis, command terms or the written explanation.

We review the student's course information, current material, recent marked work where available and assessment dates. This avoids common mismatches such as practising a harder-looking question from the wrong option, revising a topic that is not the immediate source of lost marks, or treating a communication issue as a knowledge gap.

The outcome is a working route: the concept that must be rebuilt, the type of question that will test it and the evidence that will show the method is now secure.

## The Método Nebula lesson cycle

Each session is built around visible mathematical or scientific decisions rather than a long explanation followed by unconnected homework.

1. **Diagnose the current obstacle.** We use a short question, an attempted solution or an extract from the student's course to locate the first incorrect assumption.
2. **Reconstruct the model.** The tutor explains the underlying relationship, notation and conditions under which a method works.
3. **Work one example transparently.** Each line is justified: units, diagram, algebra, graph or chemical equation are part of the reasoning, not decoration.
4. **Transfer the method.** The student solves a changed version independently, with feedback focused on the exact decision rather than on a vague “be careful”.
5. **Set the next Campus route.** Practice is selected by concept and difficulty so that the session leads to purposeful work between lessons.

This is particularly useful in IB subjects because a correct final number is rarely enough on its own. A Physics calculation needs a defensible model and units; a Chemistry answer may need a balanced equation and a justified conclusion; a Mathematics solution must communicate why the chosen method applies.

## An IB Mathematics example

Consider a student who can use a calculator for regression but cannot defend the model. The session does not begin by repeating calculator buttons. We first inspect a scatter plot, identify the variable meanings and compare a linear model with the residual pattern. The student then interprets the gradient in context, states what $r^2$ can and cannot show, and explains why extrapolation would be unsafe outside the observed range.

The related Campus material provides a clear sequence: model selection, interpretation, residuals and original worked examples. It means the student can revisit the exact chain without relying on memory of a single lesson. ## An IB science example

In Physics, a weak experimental conclusion often comes from treating uncertainty as an afterthought. In a session, the student may calculate a derived quantity, combine percentage uncertainties, plot error bars and interpret the gradient of a graph. The goal is not to manufacture certainty; it is to write a conclusion that matches the quality of the measurements.

In Chemistry, a titration error often begins before the final formula: a volume is left in cm$^3$, the equation is not balanced, or a 1:2 ratio is treated as 1:1. We work through the chain from chemical equation to amount of substance to conclusion, then use the Campus to practise variations of that same chain.
