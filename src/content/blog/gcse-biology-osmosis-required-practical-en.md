The potato-cylinder osmosis practical is easy to perform badly if you focus only on the final graph. GCSE exam questions often ask why mass changed, why percentage change is better than raw mass, and how the method becomes a fair test.

> **Direct answer.** Water moves by osmosis through a partially permeable membrane from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution. In potato cylinders, measure the percentage change in mass after placing equal cylinders in different sugar or salt concentrations.

## The core mechanism

Potato cells contain a cell membrane that is partially permeable. If the surrounding solution has a higher water potential than the cell sap, water enters the cells and mass increases. If the solution is more concentrated, water leaves the cells and mass decreases.

Avoid saying that solute moves by osmosis. Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules.

## A practical method that answers the question

1. Use a cork borer to cut potato cylinders of equal diameter.
2. Trim them to the same length and blot them consistently before weighing.
3. Record their initial masses.
4. Put cylinders into a range of known solution concentrations for the same time.
5. Remove, blot in the same way and record final masses.
6. Repeat each concentration and calculate a mean.

The independent variable is solution concentration. The dependent variable is percentage change in mass. Controls include potato source, cylinder size, volume of solution, temperature, immersion time and blotting method.

## Why calculate percentage change?

Cylinders do not always start at exactly the same mass. Percentage change makes outcomes comparable:

$$
\text{percentage change}=
\frac{\text{final mass}-\text{initial mass}}{\text{initial mass}}\times100.
$$

A cylinder rising from 2.0 g to 2.2 g and one rising from 4.0 g to 4.4 g have the same 10% increase, even though their raw changes differ.

## Reading the graph

Plot concentration on the x-axis and mean percentage change in mass on the y-axis. The concentration where the line crosses zero is the approximate isotonic point: there is no net movement of water because the solution and cell sap have similar water potential.

Do not describe this as «no osmosis». Water still moves both ways; the net change is zero.

## Evaluation that is specific

- Use the same potato or similar tissue because different potatoes can have different solute concentrations.
- Use more concentration values near the zero-crossing to estimate the isotonic point more precisely.
- Blot every cylinder with the same pressure and time; surface water changes the measured mass.
- Use repeats, calculate a mean and identify anomalies with evidence.
- Keep the volume of solution large enough that concentration does not change greatly.

## Common mark-losing statements

- «Water moves from high concentration to low concentration» without saying what is concentrated: water or solute.
- Calling the cell wall partially permeable instead of the cell membrane.
- Giving only one control variable without explaining how it is controlled.
- Using raw mass change where cylinders started with different masses.
- Saying zero change means no molecules move.
