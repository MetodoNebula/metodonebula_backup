GCSE Energy Changes: Reaction Profiles, Bonds and Calorimetry Explained Precisely

Learn GCSE energy changes: exothermic and endothermic reactions, profiles, activation energy, bond energies and calorimetry.

Saying that a reaction “releases heat” is not enough for a strong GCSE answer. You need to distinguish the system from the surroundings, interpret energy levels and explain why breaking bonds absorbs energy while making bonds releases it.

Direct answer. A reaction is exothermic when energy transfers from the system to the surroundings and ΔH<0\Delta H<0ΔH<0; it is endothermic when the system takes in energy and ΔH>0\Delta H>0ΔH>0. Activation energy is the initial barrier, not the overall energy change.

Exothermic or endothermic

In an exothermic reaction, the temperature of the surroundings usually rises. Combustion, neutralisation and many oxidation reactions are examples. Products lie at a lower energy than reactants.

In an endothermic reaction, the surroundings usually cool down. Some thermal decompositions and instant cold packs are examples. Products lie at a higher energy.

The sign refers to the system:

ΔH=Hproducts−Hreactants.\Delta H=H_{products}-H_{reactants}.ΔH=Hproducts​−Hreactants​.

Reading an energy profile

The vertical axis represents energy and the horizontal axis represents reaction progress. The vertical difference between reactants and the peak is the activation energy EaE_aEa​. The difference between products and reactants is ΔH\Delta HΔH.

Do not calculate ΔH\Delta HΔH from the peak. The peak represents a temporary transition state; it is neither a reactant nor a product.

Breaking and making bonds

Breaking bonds requires energy. Making bonds releases energy. A bond-energy estimate is:

ΔH=∑E(bonds broken)−∑E(bonds formed).\Delta H=\sum E(\text{bonds broken})-\sum E(\text{bonds formed}).ΔH=∑E(bonds broken)−∑E(bonds formed).

If bond formation releases more energy than bond breaking absorbs, the result is negative and the reaction is exothermic.

Bond-energy example

For a reaction that absorbs 620 kJ/mol620\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}620 kJ/mol to break bonds and releases 790 kJ/mol790\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}790 kJ/mol when new bonds form:

ΔH=620−790=−170 kJ/mol.\Delta H=620-790=-170\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}.ΔH=620−790=−170 kJ/mol.

The negative sign and per-mole unit are part of the answer. Writing only 170 kJ170\ \mathrm{kJ}170 kJ is incomplete.

Calorimetry

Energy transferred to water or a solution is approximated by:

q=mcΔT.q=mc\Delta T.q=mcΔT.

mmm is normally in grams, ccc in J g−1 ∘C−1\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,^{\circ}C^{-1}}Jg−1∘C−1 and ΔT\Delta TΔT in degrees Celsius. If molar energy is required:

ΔH=−qn.\Delta H=-\frac{q}{n}.ΔH=−nq​.

The sign changes because heat gained by the surroundings was lost by the reaction.

Catalysts

A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower EaE_aEa​. It increases the proportion of collisions able to react, but does not change ΔH\Delta HΔH, the initial and final energy levels or the equilibrium position.

On a profile, the catalysed curve has a lower peak and the same starting and finishing points.

Evaluating an experiment

School calorimetry usually underestimates energy released because heat escapes to the air and container, combustion may be incomplete or fuel may evaporate. Sensible improvements include insulation, a lid, a shorter flame-to-container distance and more precise measurement.

A strong evaluation links each limitation to the direction of its effect instead of simply writing “human error”.
