Waves, electric fields and circuits use compact formulas, but the interpretation comes from the quantities around them: direction, reference potential, phase, units and the assumptions of a model.

Direct answer. Use v=fλv=f\lambdav=fλ for a wave only after identifying the medium and what changes at a boundary. In electricity, distinguish potential difference, current and power before selecting an equation. In fields, state the direction as well as the magnitude.

Waves: speed, frequency and wavelength

v=fλ.v=f\lambda.v=fλ.

Frequency is set by the source. When a wave enters a new medium, its speed and wavelength can change while its frequency remains the same. If a 12 Hz wave travels at 3.6 m s−1^{-1}−1, then λ=3.6/12=0.30\lambda=3.6/12=0.30λ=3.6/12=0.30 m.

For diffraction and interference, a numerical answer needs a diagram or a path difference argument. A formula alone does not identify constructive or destructive interference.

Potential difference and electrical power

Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge:

V=WQ.V=\frac{W}{Q}.V=QW​.

Electrical power can be written as

P=IV,P=IV,P=IV,

so a 0.40 A current through a 12 V component transfers energy at 4.8 W. The unit watt is joule per second; checking that link helps catch incorrect substitutions.

Electric fields and potential

For a point charge, the magnitude of the electric field is

E=14πε0∣Q∣r2.E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|Q|}{r^2}.E=4πε0​1​r2∣Q∣​.

The field direction is away from a positive source charge and toward a negative source charge. Do not confuse the direction of the field with the force on a negative test charge; that force points opposite to the field.

Worked problem: voltage, energy and charge

A charge of 3.0 mC3.0\ \mathrm{mC}3.0 mC passes through a potential difference of 24 V24\ \mathrm{V}24 V. The energy transferred is

W=QV=(3.0×10−3)(24)=7.2×10−2 J.W=QV=(3.0\times10^{-3})(24)=7.2\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{J}.W=QV=(3.0×10−3)(24)=7.2×10−2 J.

The prefix conversion is the decisive step. Using 3.03.03.0 instead of 3.0×10−33.0\times10^{-3}3.0×10−3 would make the answer one thousand times too large.

What a graph can tell you

For a uniform electric field, a graph of potential difference against distance has a constant gradient whose magnitude is the field strength. For a non-uniform field around a point charge, neither field strength nor potential changes linearly with distance. Identify the model before interpreting a gradient.

A wave boundary calculation

A 500 Hz500\ \mathrm{Hz}500 Hz wave enters a material where its speed falls from 340 m s−1340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}340 ms−1 to 250 m s−1250\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}250 ms−1. The source fixes the frequency, so it remains 500 Hz500\ \mathrm{Hz}500 Hz. Its new wavelength is

λ=vf=250500=0.50 m.\lambda=\frac{v}{f}=\frac{250}{500}=0.50\ \mathrm{m}.λ=fv​=500250​=0.50 m.

The old wavelength was 0.68 m0.68\ \mathrm{m}0.68 m, so the wavelength changes with the speed. This separates a physical argument from a formula substitution and avoids the common, incorrect claim that frequency changes at the boundary.

Reading data safely

  • Write prefixes explicitly: mA is 10−310^{-3}10−3 A and mV is 10−310^{-3}10−3 V.
  • Keep wavelength in metres when using SI wave speed.
  • State a potential reference when interpreting electric potential.
  • Give field and force directions with words, arrows or signed axes.
  • Field strength is N C−1^{-1}−1 or V m−1^{-1}−1, whereas potential is J C−1^{-1}−1.
