Motors and generators often look like separate topics. They are actually the same interaction viewed in opposite directions: a current in a magnetic field can produce motion, while motion that changes magnetic flux can produce a voltage.

Direct answer. In a motor, current and magnetic field produce a force on a conductor. In a generator, relative motion between a conductor and a field induces a potential difference. The exam requires you to identify what changes, apply the correct direction and explain the energy transfer.

Magnetic field and current

Field lines leave the north pole and enter the south pole. Their spacing represents strength: closer lines mean a stronger field. A current also creates a field around its conductor; around a straight wire it forms concentric circles.

In a solenoid, the fields from individual turns combine to produce a pattern similar to a bar magnet. The field becomes stronger when current increases, more turns are added per unit length or an iron core is inserted.

Force on a conductor

A conductor of length LLL carrying current III in a field BBB experiences, when perpendicular:

F=BIL.F=BIL.F=BIL.

More generally:

F=BILsin⁡θ.F=BIL\sin\theta.F=BILsinθ.

The force is greatest at 90∘90^\circ90∘ and zero when the conductor is parallel to the field. Its direction is perpendicular to both current and field; use the direction rule required by your specification rather than guessing from the drawing.

The motor effect

A rectangular coil has opposite forces on its two sides. Their rotational effects do not cancel because they act along different lines: they form a couple that turns the coil.

In a direct-current motor, the split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn so the turning effect keeps the same direction. Brushes maintain electrical contact. Without that reversal, the coil would tend to stop and turn back.

Induction and generators

A voltage is induced when the magnetic flux linking a conductor changes. This can happen because the magnet moves, the coil rotates, field strength varies or the effective area changes.

A faster change produces a larger voltage. A stronger field and more turns also increase it. The induced current produces an effect that opposes the change causing it, which is why generating electricity requires mechanical work.

Transformers

An alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing field in the core. That change induces a voltage in the secondary:

Common exam mistakes

  • Confusing magnetic field with magnetic force.
  • Drawing field lines that cross.
  • Claiming that a steady field alone induces voltage without any change.
  • Forgetting that a transformer requires alternating current.
  • Explaining a generator without describing mechanical-to-electrical energy transfer.
